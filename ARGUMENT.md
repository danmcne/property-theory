# Co-ownership is not a contradiction

*The case for the exclusion view of ownership, with reference to the formal theory in this repository. The technical account of the axioms, theorems and models is in [README.md](README.md) and [THEORY.md](THEORY.md). Names in `code` refer to theorems and scenarios checked there.*

## 0. The claim and why it matters

A recurring claim among libertarians is that two persons cannot own the same thing — that ownership is, of its nature, held by one person. LiquidZulu states it without qualification in his course on property: "ownership is necessarily individual—that is, group ownership is strictly impossible." Stephan Kinsella, answering a reader who put the same view to him, records it in the form most people meet it: "if co-owners disagree on the use of a property, one of them must win the conflict, and the winner is therefore the owner."

If the claim were true, a great deal of ordinary life would have to be redescribed. Two friends who buy a boat together would not own it; a couple who buy a house would not own it; three heirs would not inherit an estate, but would each hold something else, perhaps a contract over property that one of them alone owns. LiquidZulu accepts this consequence for firms: "Just as a person cannot own friendship or marriage they cannot own a firm," and whatever a firm directs must, on his account, be owned by a single individual. The claim therefore decides what a libertarian may say about partnerships, households, inheritance and joint purchase.

I will argue that the claim is true of one concept of ownership and false of another, and that libertarian theory needs the second. On the first concept — ownership as *exclusive control*, or as LiquidZulu puts it, "direction" — two owners of one thing are a contradiction in terms, and the argument against co-ownership is valid. On the second — ownership as *the right to exclude* — two owners of one thing are perfectly consistent, and the argument against co-ownership rests on a premise that, stated generally, contradicts sole ownership as well. The formal theory in this repository makes both halves of this checkable: it shows that a full libertarian theory of property built on the right to exclude has models containing co-owners, and it shows exactly which premise, when added, produces the contradiction.

## 1. The argument against co-ownership, at full strength

LiquidZulu's version is the clearest, and I give it in his terms. Ownership, he says, "is just direction": to own a thing is to be the one whose will directs its use. Suppose a set of persons each own a stick in common, and two of them, A and B, come into conflict over its use. Then "either A is said to be the just victor, or B is." If A, "then he owns the stick and B does not"; if B, the reverse. Either outcome would "contradict the presumption that every member in the set owned the stick." Group ownership therefore cannot occur.

The obvious reply — let the owners vote — he anticipates: "any who lost the vote did not own the stick, as their desired possession was considered unjust." And he forestalls the reply that owners usually agree, on the ground that the law concerns itself only with situations of conflict. Where there is no conflict, no rule is needed; where there is conflict, someone wins, and the winner is the owner.

The argument has three premises. First, ownership is direction: the owner is the one whose will settles the use. Second, every conflict between two persons over a thing has a just victor. Third, the victor owns and the loser does not. The conclusion follows validly. If the argument fails, one of these premises is false.

## 2. Two senses of "exclusive"

The argument draws its force from an ambiguity in the word *exclusive*. Libertarians say that property is exclusive, and they mean two different things by it.

In the first sense, exclusive control means that *this person, to the exclusion of all others, may control and use the thing*. The exclusion falls on the use: one will settles it, and every other will is shut out. In the second sense, ownership is *a right to exclude all others*. The exclusion falls on other persons: the owner may forbid them, and nothing is said about whose use goes ahead.

The difference is the one Hohfeld drew between a claim and a privilege. A right to exclude is a claim: it puts others under a duty not to use the thing without the owner's consent. Exclusive control adds a privilege — the owner is under no duty to refrain — and protects that privilege against interference. Two persons can each hold a claim against the other over the same thing; the two duties are jointly satisfiable, since both can be met by neither using it. Two persons cannot each hold exclusive control in the first sense, because A's protected privilege to direct the use is B's duty to defer, and B's protected privilege is A's duty to defer. In a conflict, each would be both bound and free with respect to the same act. That is a contradiction, and it is exactly the one the stick argument finds.

The formal theory states the exclusion concept directly. Its only normative primitive is a *title*: the right of one person to exclude one other person from using one thing in one manner. A *full owner* holds a title against every other person in every manner. *Co-owners* are two or more full owners of the same thing; each holds a title against the others. A person may use a thing in a manner unless someone who holds a title against him, over that thing and in that manner, does not consent. Nothing in the theory gives an owner a privilege to use.

The exclusive-control concept can be added to this theory as two contrast axioms: an owner may use his property in any manner, and no other person may use it in a way that clashes with what the owner wants. With both added, the provers derive that two co-owners of a scarce thing can never want clashing uses of it (`control_coownership_contradiction`). Since nothing in libertarian theory constrains what a person may want, co-ownership becomes impossible. Remove either axiom, or let the protection run only against non-owners, and co-ownership has models again (`co_ownership_with_use_liberty_only`, `co_ownership_with_protection_only`, `co_ownership_with_protection_against_outsiders`). The contradiction belongs to exclusive control, not to co-ownership.

## 3. The premise that fails: every conflict has a just victor

Under the exclusion view, a conflict between co-owners has a determinate rightful outcome, and it is neither of the two the stick argument allows. Suppose Ada and Ben co-own a field. Ada wants to plow it; Ben wants to sow it; the two uses cannot happen together; and each refuses the other. Then Ada may not plow, because Ben holds a title against her and does not consent, and Ben may not sow, for the same reason in reverse (`joint_deadlock`). Neither is the just victor. The field lies idle until they agree.

This outcome satisfies every title in play. Ada's claim against Ben is honored, since Ben does not sow; Ben's claim against Ada is honored, since Ada does not plow. Nobody's will has been overridden and nobody has been wronged. The model finders confirm that the full theory — homesteading, transfer, abandonment, defence and all — has models in which this happens and no one commits aggression (`co_ownership_consistent`). The stick argument's second premise lists two outcomes; the exclusion view supplies a third, and the third is the one that its rules actually select.

The premise was therefore not an innocent description of what conflict resolution requires. It assumed that a rightful resolution must award the use to someone. The exclusion view denies this: a rightful resolution must tell each party what he may not do, and "neither of you without the other" is such an answer.

## 4. The premise, stated generally, proves too much

There is a further difficulty with the just-victor premise, and it is decisive. As stated, it concerns every conflict over a thing between two persons. Take it at its word and add it to the theory as an axiom: whenever two persons want clashing uses of a thing, one of them may make his use and the other may not.

Now consider a sole owner. Ada alone owns a field. Ben wants to plow it and Cy wants to sow it, and the uses clash. Ada refuses both. Under the exclusion view, both are forbidden, and Ada's field stays as she wishes. Under the just-victor premise, one of Ben and Cy must be permitted. Ada's refusal must give way. The provers derive this as a theorem: with the premise added, an owner can never refuse two rivals who want clashing uses of his property (`direction_overreach`). No libertarian accepts that conclusion. The premise, stated generally, contradicts sole ownership.

So the premise must be restricted — to conflicts between owners, say. But restricted in that way it says that co-owners can never be in a deadlock, and the provers confirm that this is exactly what it does (`direction_forbids_deadlock`). The restricted premise is not a neutral principle of conflict resolution from which the impossibility of co-ownership follows; it is the impossibility of co-ownership, stated as a premise. The argument is valid only because its conclusion has been written into one of its premises.

## 5. The winner is not the owner

The third premise — that whoever prevails in a conflict is the owner — survives in a subtler form, and Kinsella's correspondent puts it well: if co-owners disagree, one must win, and the winner is the owner. In the deadlock of section 3, it may be said, Ada wanted the field idle all along, and the field is idle; so Ada's will prevails, and Ada is the real owner.

The theory makes "prevails" precise. A person *controls* a thing when he may use it in every manner and every other person may use it exactly when he consents. Two theorems follow. A sole full owner who is not aggressing against anyone controls his property (`sole_owner_controls`). A co-owner controls the thing only if every other co-owner consents to every use he makes (`control_needs_deference`). Now return to Ada. At one instant she wants the field idle and Ben wants to plow; both refuse each other; the field is idle and every want of Ada's is met. At the next instant Ada wants to sow, and Ben still refuses. Ada cannot sow. At neither instant does Ada control the field, and neither does Ben (`idleness_is_not_control`).

Ada's wish for idleness prevailed only because refraining requires no one's consent. That is a feature of every rule of unanimity: the party who wants least gets his way, because the others' consents are not needed for nothing to happen. It confers no power over anyone's use, Ada's own included. The outcome matched her wish; it did not obey her will. The voting objection fails in the same way. A co-owner whose proposal loses under a decision rule that the co-owners agreed beforehand still holds every title he held before. By agreeing to the rule he consented in advance to the uses it selects; he exercised his titles when he agreed, and did not lose them. (Consent that lasts over time is outside the present formal theory; the point here does not depend on it.)

## 6. Why libertarian theory needs the right to exclude

That co-ownership is consistent under the exclusion concept would matter little if libertarians had independent reason to prefer exclusive control. They have reason to prefer the opposite, for four connected reasons.

First, libertarian rights are negative. The non-aggression principle is a prohibition: it tells each person what he must not do to what belongs to others. A norm of that kind takes the form of a claim against others' use; it cannot take the form of a guarantee that the owner's use will go ahead, because no norm can guarantee a use. Scarcity limits use — that is what scarcity is — and so do the titles of one's neighbours. The theory records the second limit as a theorem: a person may use his own knife, but an action that also uses another person's body without consent is aggression, whoever owns the knife (`ownership_is_not_licence`). Even a sole owner, then, has no unconditional privilege to use his property. The privilege is what remains after everyone else's claims, and the exclusion view says so from the start.

Second, the exclusion view does the work that libertarians assign to property. LiquidZulu himself describes property rights as "conflict avoiding norms." Under the exclusion view every conflict over an owned thing has a determinate rightful answer for each party, namely what he may not do; and everyone can comply at once, since refraining never violates anyone's title (`refraining_innocent`). A set of rights that can always be satisfied together is exactly what a conflict-avoiding norm should be. The deadlock between co-owners is one such answer, not a gap in the norm.

Third, the exclusion view recovers exclusive control where exclusive control is true. A sole full owner controls his property: he may use it as he likes, so far as that thing alone is concerned, and others may use it exactly when he consents (`sole_owner_controls`). The direction view describes this case accurately. Its error is to take the commonest case for the definition, and so to rule out by definition every arrangement in which the case does not hold.

Fourth, the exclusion view handles ordinary transactions without special axioms. In the formal theory, a title passes by an accepted grant, and everything else is defined from grants: a share gift makes the recipient a co-owner (`share_gift`); a transfer to two buyers at once is a joint purchase that makes them co-owners (`joint_purchase`); a lease transfers the titles in some manners and leaves the landlord the rest (`lease_tenant_excludes_landlord`, `lessor_loses_manner`); an easement releases one person from one manner (`easement_use`). Two persons who homestead a field together become co-owners by the ordinary homesteading rule (`joint_homesteading`). None of this needs an axiom about co-ownership. Under the direction view, each of these arrangements has to be explained away as something other than ownership.

## 7. Objections

**"An owner who may not use his property owns nothing."** This assumes that ownership guarantees use, and section 6 showed that it never does. A sole owner may not use his land in a way that uses his neighbour's; a co-owner may not use the common land in a way his co-owner refuses. In both cases the owner keeps what ownership consists in — the right to exclude others — and in both cases his use depends on others' titles. The co-owner is not a lesser owner; he is an owner whose neighbours include someone who holds the same thing.

**"A rule that can leave a field idle indefinitely fails at its purpose."** Deadlock is a real cost, and I do not dispute it. It is the reason sole ownership is usually the better arrangement, and the reason sensible co-owners agree in advance how they will decide, or how one may buy out the other. But a cost is not a contradiction. A sole owner may also leave his field idle indefinitely, and no libertarian thinks this impugns his ownership. The co-owners chose their arrangement, whether by homesteading together, by buying together, or by gift; they can leave it by agreement, by sale, or by abandonment. Kinsella goes further and treats division or sale as the default when co-owners disagree. The exclusion view does not need that default, and nothing in the logic of property supplies a right to force a partition on an unwilling co-owner. Some things cannot be divided at all, or only in time.

**"The co-owners are really one owner — the group."** Kinsella describes co-owners as "conceptually one unit—'the owner'—the entity that can deny or grant permission to others to use the resource." That is a useful description, but the formal theory needs no such entity. Every title in it is held by a person against a person. What looks from outside like a unit is a pattern: each co-owner holds a title against every outsider, so an outsider needs every co-owner's consent (`unanimity`), and each co-owner holds a title against every other. Nothing is owned by a group; several persons each own.

**"Legal co-ownership works differently, and it works."** It does work differently. In most legal systems each co-owner may use the common thing without the others' consent: the Italian civil code permits each to use it provided he does not prevent the others from making equal use of it (art. 1102), and the common law treats the exclusion of one co-tenant by another as a wrong. This "free use" can be read in two ways, and on neither reading does it settle the conflicts that property exists to settle.

Read as a bare permission, it is consistent but empty where it matters. If Ada wants to plow and Ben wants to sow, both uses are permitted and at most one can happen (`free_use_unsettled`); the titles allow either outcome, and which use goes ahead depends on who acts first (`free_use_ada_acts_first`, `free_use_ben_acts_first`). If Ada has planted corn, Ben is as free to plow it under as she was to plant it. Read instead as a guarantee, with each co-owner's use protected against the other's interference, it is the control view of section 2 confined to co-owners, and when their uses clash it promises each of them what it forbids to the other. Either way, the parties cannot both have the free use the law appears to grant them, and who may really use the thing has to be decided by a majority, a court or a sale. That is bad law: it sends co-owners to an outside decision whenever they disagree, unless their own contract is better than the law. The veto form settles every such conflict by the titles alone, since two co-owners' uses are both permitted only if each consents to the other's (`veto_settles_conflict`). Under it Ben may not plow without Ada's agreement, and the corn is safe without any special rule about crops. When the co-owners do not agree, the answer is that neither may proceed, which is a definite answer that both can obey.

**"Defining ownership as the right to exclude simply begs the question the other way."** It would, if consistency had been obtained by definition. It was not. The claim that co-ownership is consistent is a claim about a whole theory — homesteading, acquisition only from earlier titles, transfer, abandonment, aggression and defence — and model finders check it against all of those axioms together. And the choice of concept was argued in section 6 on grounds that do not mention co-ownership at all. What the formal work cannot do is settle which concept is correct; that rests on the argument of section 6, which a reader may reject. A reader who rejects it must then hold a concept of ownership under which an owner's refusal can be overridden by two rivals (section 4), or else accept the restricted premise, which assumes the conclusion.

## 8. What has been shown, and what has not

The formal theory establishes three things. A libertarian theory of property built on the right to exclude, with homesteading, transfer by accepted grant, abandonment, aggression and defence, is consistent; three independent methods exhibit models of it, and some of those models contain co-owners who disagree and wrong no one. Exclusive control, added to that theory, makes co-ownership contradictory, and the contradiction is the Hohfeldian clash of one co-owner's claim with the other's protected privilege. The just-victor premise, added to the theory, contradicts sole ownership unless it is restricted; restricted, it is the conclusion it was meant to support.

It does not establish that the right to exclude is the correct concept of ownership; that is argued, not proved. Nor does it settle what co-owners owe each other by contract, how a lease ends, what an aggressor owes his victim, or how much force a victim may use; the formal theory is silent on all of these, and its limits are listed in [README.md](README.md).

The argument against co-ownership, then, is not wrong in its logic. It is right about exclusive control, and exclusive control is not what libertarians should mean by ownership. An owner is someone who may exclude others. Two persons may each be that, with respect to one thing, at one time; when they disagree, the thing waits.
