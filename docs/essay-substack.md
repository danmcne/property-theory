# Two owners, one field

A claim circulates among libertarians that two people cannot own the same thing. LiquidZulu puts it flatly in his course on property: "ownership is necessarily individual—that is, group ownership is strictly impossible." The usual argument is short. Suppose A and B own a stick together and disagree about its use. Then "either A is said to be the just victor, or B is." Whoever wins owns the stick; whoever loses does not; so they did not both own it.

If this were right, much of ordinary life would need redescribing. Friends who buy a boat together would not own it, nor would a couple who buy a house, nor three heirs who inherit a farm. LiquidZulu accepts the consequence for firms, which on his account cannot own anything; whatever a firm directs must belong to one individual. The claim decides what a libertarian may say about households, partnerships and inheritance, so it is worth getting right.

I think it is right about one idea of ownership and wrong about another, and that libertarians should hold the other one.

## Two meanings of "exclusive"

Libertarians say property is exclusive, and they mean two different things.

On the first meaning, ownership is *exclusive control*: this person, to the exclusion of everyone else, may direct and use the thing. LiquidZulu defines ownership this way — "ownership is just direction." On the second meaning, ownership is *a right to exclude*: the owner may forbid others to use the thing, and nothing is promised about whose use goes ahead.

Wesley Hohfeld's distinction between a claim and a privilege shows where the difference lies. A right to exclude is a *claim*: it puts others under a duty not to use the thing without your consent. Exclusive control adds a *privilege* — you are under no such duty yourself — and protects that privilege against interference. Two people can each hold a claim against the other over one field; both duties are met if neither uses it without the other's consent. Two people cannot each hold exclusive control, because A's protected privilege is B's duty to defer and B's is A's. In a disagreement each would be both bound and free with respect to the same act. That is a genuine contradiction, and it is the one the stick argument finds.

So the argument is valid — for exclusive control. The question is whether that is what ownership is.

## The missing outcome

Return to the stick, and read ownership as the right to exclude. A and B each hold a claim against the other. A wants to use it one way, B another, and each refuses the other. What follows? A may not use it, because B forbids it; B may not use it, because A forbids it. Neither is the "just victor." The stick waits until they agree.

That outcome violates no one's rights. A's claim is honored, since B does not use the stick; B's claim is honored, since A does not. The argument offered two possible verdicts — A wins, or B wins — and the right to exclude supplies a third: neither, without the other. The premise that every conflict must have a just victor was doing all the work.

## The premise proves too much

That premise has a worse problem. Taken as stated, it is about every conflict between two people over a thing — and it then destroys sole ownership too. Suppose Ada alone owns a field. Ben wants to plow it, Cy wants to sow it, the two uses clash, and Ada refuses both. If every conflict must have a just victor, one of Ben and Cy must be permitted, and Ada's refusal has to give way. No libertarian believes that.

So the premise has to be restricted to conflicts between owners. But restricted that way, it simply says that co-owners can never be at an impasse — which is the conclusion it was supposed to prove.

## "But whoever wins is the real owner"

A subtler version runs: if A wants the field idle and B wants to farm it, the field stays idle, so A's will prevails and A is the real owner.

It is true that A got what A wanted. But A's wish prevailed only because doing nothing requires nobody's consent. The next season A wants to sow, B still refuses, and A cannot sow. A never controlled the field; the outcome matched one wish, once. Under any rule that requires everyone's agreement, the person who wants least gets his way, because no one needs permission for nothing to happen. That gives him no power over anyone's use, his own included.

The right-to-exclude view still accounts for control. A sole owner — someone whom nobody else can exclude, and who can exclude everyone else — does control his property: he may use it as he likes, so far as the thing itself is concerned, and others may use it exactly when he says so. Exclusive control is what ownership looks like in its commonest case. The mistake is to make the commonest case the definition.

## Why the right to exclude is the right definition

Libertarian rights are negative. The non-aggression principle tells each of us what we must not do to what belongs to others; it guarantees no one's use of anything. It could not, because scarcity limits use and so do your neighbours' rights. You may use your own knife, but not in a way that uses my body. Even a sole owner's "privilege to use" is only what is left over after everyone else's claims. Defining ownership as the right to exclude says this from the start.

It also does the job property is for. LiquidZulu calls property rights "conflict avoiding norms." Under the right to exclude, every conflict over an owned thing has a definite answer for each party — what he may not do — and everyone can obey at once, because refraining never violates anyone's rights. A deadlock between co-owners is one of those answers, not a hole in the rules.

Deadlock is a real cost. That is why sole ownership is usually the better arrangement, and why sensible co-owners agree in advance how they will decide, or how one can buy the other out. But a cost is not a contradiction, and a sole owner may leave his field idle for years without anyone doubting that he owns it.

The law's usual alternative is worse. Most legal systems give each co-owner free use of the whole. That looks generous and settles nothing. If Ada and Ben are each free to use the field and Ada has planted corn, Ben is as free to plow it under as she was to plant it. Either the freedom is a bare permission, in which case whoever acts first wins, or it is a guarantee, in which case the law has promised each of them what it forbids to the other. They cannot both have free use, so the question of who may really use the field goes to a vote or a judge. Under the right to exclude, Ben may not plow without Ada's agreement, and the corn is safe without any special rule.

## What the formal check shows

I did not want to rest this on prose alone, so I wrote the theory down as axioms in formal logic — homesteading, transfer by accepted grant, leases, easements, abandonment, aggression and defence — and had two independent automated provers check the theorems and three independent methods look for models. The theory is consistent, and it has models in which two co-owners disagree, refuse each other, and nobody wrongs anyone. Add exclusive control, as two extra axioms, and co-ownership of a scarce thing becomes contradictory. Add the "just victor" premise instead, and a sole owner can no longer refuse two rivals. The details, the proofs and a longer version of this argument are in the repository.

None of this proves that the right to exclude is the correct idea of ownership; that is an argument, and you may reject it. But if you reject it, you need an idea of ownership under which an owner's refusal can be overruled by two strangers who want his field — or a premise that assumes co-ownership is impossible in order to show that it is.

An owner is someone who may exclude others. Two people can each be that, with respect to one thing, at the same time. When they disagree, the thing waits.
